My research examines the history and politics of technological modernity. I am particularly interested in the ways the material infrastructures of the modern world embody and uphold relations of power and domination, despite promising to liberate humanity from scarcity and toil.

My doctoral work centered on the _longue durée_ of industrial modernity and what the early Frankfurt School theorists called “instrumental reason,” tracing its roots to the nineteenth century. My dissertation, _Karl Marx, Philosopher of Technics: Critique, Alienation, and the Materiality of Social Power_, grounded this inquiry in mid-19th-century debates on technology and political economy that decisively shaped the first appraisals of industrialization, including Marx's central intervention. Drawing on understudied materials from the _Marx-Engels Gesamtausgabe_ (MEGA²), I&nbsp;reconstructed how Marx came to understand the relationship between capitalism and the modern technical system, against the backdrop of a techno-determinist common sense growing out of the Industrial Revolution. Contrary to prevailing economicist and productivist readings, I argued that Marx theorized industrial development and its constitutive mechanism -- the “subsumption” of the labor process by capital -- as an alienated organization of human-technical relations, whereby capitalist power becomes embedded in the very conditions of human life.

As I revise this work into a monograph, my ongoing research traces how the core dynamic of subsumption has steered the course of the information age and is reasserting itself in the contemporary AI project -- especially in its claim to expand human autonomy through the automation of judgment and reason. 

Since the postwar period, the digital computer has been celebrated for reintroducing human feedback into the unthinking, mechanical systems of industrial modernity, making external input and self-regulation integral to machine operations. In so doing, the advent of information technologies seemingly reversed the erosion of reflexivity that 20th-century critics -- from phenomenology and existentialism to early critical theory -- ascribed to mechanization and the reign of instrumental reason. Yet this optimistic self-image of the digital age has consistently faced scrutiny within media and technology studies -- most pointedly in light of Gilles Deleuze's late concept of “control societies," which directly linked the rise of the computer to a new diagram of power. Building on my previous inquiry into the relationship between social and technical forms, my current project takes up, more explicitly, the relationship between the industrial form of the labor process and computation, turning to the 20th-century history of computing, postwar philosophical appraisals of machine intelligence, and the ideological afterlives of the cybernetics movement. Through this constellation, I&nbsp;aim to ask how the ontology of computing machines is bound up with the sociality of capitalist life, and whether the socialized intelligence they harness truly foretells its overcoming.

## Work in Progress: Computation and Social Form

What is a computational system and what form of human grouping begets its existence? Or put another way, what is the form in which modern humans confront materiality such that it requires an automated system of control and communication?

A computer is, on most modern definitions, a system that performs sequences of mathematical or logical operations on inputs, according to rules specified in advance. So defined, many technical systems preceding the digital electronic computer would qualify as “computational,” from the Jacquard loom to the group of human calculators employed in the production of logarithmic tables until the late nineteenth century. Yet it is only under conditions of advanced industrial development that computational systems have come to organize the entirety of human existence, embedding information-processing operations at every level of the modern lifeworld. From scientific research, manufacturing, and the circulation of goods and people to the tracking and management of consumer desires, behaviors, and political sentiments, there is no aspect of contemporary life that is not either directly or indirectly mediated by computational processes. It is, therefore, unsurprising that the digital has come to function as a technological synecdoche for late capitalism itself, as a new form of “enclosure” that, like its analog predecessors, abstracts and elides the heterogeneities of the material world so as to render it exploitable. 

Yet is control and domination by logico-mathematical principles all there is to computational systems and the machinic modes of intelligence they enact? While the digital computer has emerged from and ultimately serves, in its present form, capitalist accumulation, the notion that computational systems are inherently functional to that purpose deserves further investigation. As contemporary media-theoretical accounts of computation propose (Hansen, Parisi, Hui, Fazi), there is a crucial “ontological difference” between computation as a broader material-cosmological operation and the computer-regulated networks of communication and control it has become narrowly associated with. In this view, the latter does not exhaust the expansive (and potentially liberatory) ontogenetic potentials that contemporary digital technologies have unleashed, despite capitalist capture, granting 21st-century humans unprecedented access to a worldly sensibility beyond received anthropocentric norms and assumptions. 

My current research seeks to work through this tension by examining the social ontology of computation. I submit that only by grasping the human forms of relation at the basis of modern computational systems can the distinction between computation as capitalist technique and as worldly process be properly drawn. 

### _Abstraction, Mediation, and Social Ontology_
Broadly conceived, social ontology is concerned with comprehending the modes of being of the social -- that is, of that layer of “transindividual” reality that is immanent in individuals yet overflows and exceeds them, arising from their collective activity and nonetheless conditioning and constraining them. In mediating this space of constitutive relations, technology is not a passive instrument but the very medium in which humans become who they are collectively, and it is, accordingly, in the social that it finds its element.

If the social ontology of a technical system must account for the human processes at the basis of its genesis and concretization, then my working hypothesis is the following: contemporary computational systems develop within the bounds of the industrial form of the labor process, understood as a historically distinct arrangement of the interface between humans and machines arising with capitalist modernity. In this context, the term “industrial” refers not to the specific technologies and industries of a bygone Victorian or Fordist era, seemingly superseded by a de-industrializing world, but to a distinct _formal_ configuration of human self-reproduction and metabolic activity, consisting of large-scale production of commodified goods and services for the purpose of expanding capital. What distinguishes this industrial form is not, as the common understanding would have it, the productive employment of humans, machines, and nature for maximum physical output, but rather the measurement and allocation of abstract human labor-time in the most value-efficient manner. Crucially, the latter should not be confused with the profit motive that orthodox Marxism took as the principle of intelligibility of modern society. Nor does it imply that all social activity directly assumes this form, or that the concept of “industry” narrowly refers to the factory and the production of physical goods. State bureaucracies, the military-*industrial* complex, the culture _industry_, and the array of modern institutions that Michel Foucault characterized as “disciplinary” equally belong within this historical arrangement.

As historians of science and computation have argued (Daston, Galison, Schaffer, Pasquinelli), the twin birth of the computer and industrial organization suggests a common operational logic. Indeed, if the industrial coupling of humans and machines necessitates a systematic regimentation of work and value, then computation is the mechanism by which the modern technical system makes this requirement enforceable at scale. What is more, the imperative that informational processes become ever more granular, recursive, and operative across the whole technical system means that they must eventually become inscribed in the autonomous self-regulation of its parts. What thereby emerges is a form of inorganic intelligence whose technical tendency is not explained by extrinsic structural imperatives -- profitability, deskilling, labor discipline, or the rising organic composition of capital, all of which are compatible with other technical paths -- but by the operationalization of the division of labor imposed by the industrial labor process. In its drive to allocate value-producing labor with maximal efficiency and precision, large-scale industry models every productive endeavor as a sequence of rule-governed operations that can be decomposed and formalized independently of physical embodiment. To subsume an activity under the industrial form is, in this regard, to sequence its mechanical and human contributions appropriately yet without making ontological distinctions between them, as functional equivalents capable of rule-governed conduct. In fact, labor and rule-following become coextensive in this context, and machinic intelligence thus comes to rest on an algorithmic structure -- formally encapsulated by the universal Turing machine -- whose latest and most ambitious iteration is the “synthesis of word and number” (Weatherby) achieved by large language models.

The organizing questions of my ongoing research are as follows. If contemporary computational systems are the “transindividual” medium of an alienated form of cooperation based on wage labor and commodity production, do all modes of abstract mediation entail the same form of reification? In other words, does the social ontology of the modern computer necessitate oppressive forms of valuation such as the capital relation? After all, an alternative system of cooperation based on universal need satisfaction would seem to require its own means of commensuration for comparing and reconciling non-identical contents, computation thereby serving human flourishing rather than a self-moving abstraction. Whether this can be answered conclusively remains an open question I am presently contending with, but I hypothesize that the world-disclosing possibilities that contemporary computational systems already exhibit, even when enfolded in an alienated social world, call forth a form of human individuation that does not yet exist.
