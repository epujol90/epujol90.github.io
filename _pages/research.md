My research examines the history and politics of technological modernity. I am particularly interested in the ways the material infrastructures of the modern world embody and uphold relations of power and domination, despite promising to liberate humanity from scarcity and toil.

My doctoral work centered on the _longue durée_ of industrial modernity and what the early Frankfurt School theorists called “instrumental reason,” tracing its roots to the nineteenth century. My dissertation, _Karl Marx, Philosopher of Technics: Critique, Alienation, and the Materiality of Social Power_, grounded this inquiry in mid-19th-century debates on technology and political economy that decisively shaped the first appraisals of industrialization, including Marx's key intervention. Drawing on understudied materials from the _Marx-Engels Gesamtausgabe_ (MEGA²), I&nbsp;reconstructed how Marx came to understand the relationship between capitalism and the modern technical system, against the backdrop of a techno-determinist common sense growing out of the Industrial Revolution. Contrary to prevailing economicist and productivist readings, I argued that Marx theorized industrial development and its constitutive mechanism -- the “subsumption” of the labor process by capital -- as an alienated organization of human-technical relations, whereby capitalist power becomes embedded in the very conditions of human life.

As I revise this work into a monograph, my ongoing research traces how the core dynamic of subsumption has steered the course of the information age and is reasserting itself in the contemporary AI project -- especially in its claim to expand human autonomy through the automation of judgment and reason. 

Since the postwar period, the digital computer has been celebrated for reintroducing human feedback into the "unthinking" apparatuses of industrial modernity. By making external input and self-regulation integral to machine operations, the advent of information machines seemingly reversed the erosion of reflexivity that 20th-century critics -- from phenomenology and existentialism to early critical theory -- ascribed to mechanization and the reign of instrumental reason. Yet this optimistic self-image of the digital age has consistently faced scrutiny within media and technology studies -- most pointedly in light of Gilles Deleuze's late concept of “control societies," which directly linked the rise of the computer to a new diagram of power. Building on my previous inquiry into the relationship between social and technical forms, my current project takes up, more explicitly, the relationship between the industrial form of the labor process and computation, turning to the 20th-century history of computing, postwar philosophical appraisals of machine intelligence, and the ideological afterlives of the cybernetics movement. Through this constellation, I&nbsp;aim to ask how the ontology of computing machines is bound up with the sociality of capitalist life, and whether the socialized intelligence they harness truly foretells its overcoming.

### Work In Progress: Computation and Social Form

What is a computational system and what form of human grouping begets its existence? Or put another way, what is the form in which modern humans confront materiality such that it requires an automated system of universal commensuration and control?

A computer is, according to most modern definitions, a system that performs sequences of mathematical or logical operations on given inputs according to rules specified in advance. So defined, many technical systems preceding the digital electronic computer would qualify as “computational,” from the Antikythera mechanism and the Jacquard loom to the assembly line of human calculators employed in the production of logarithmic tables until the late nineteenth century. Yet it was only under conditions of advanced industrial development that automated computational systems came to organize the entirety of the human lifeworld, inserting information-processing operations at every level of modern collective self-reproduction. From scientific research and the harnessing of nature in industry to the circulation of goods and people and the tracking and management of consumer desires, behaviors, and political sentiments, there is no aspect of the capitalist social totality today that is not either directly or indirectly mediated by computational processes. The computer is, accordingly, not just a “general-purpose” technology, but the principal medium of human psycho-social individuation in commodity-producing society -- indeed, the material culmination of that mathematical “enframing” of the world that began, as the phenomenological tradition has it, with the mathematization of physics in the early modern period.

It is, therefore, unsurprising that the digital has come to function as a technological synecdoche for late capitalism itself, as a new form of “enclosure” that, like its analog predecessors, abstracts and elides the heterogeneities of the material world to render its “free” gifts into quantifiable standing reserve. Yet is control and domination by logico-mathematical principles all there is to computational systems and the machinic modes of intelligence they enact? While the digital computer has historically emerged from and ultimately serves, in its present form, capital’s process of self-expansion, the idea that computational systems are inherently functional to that alienated purpose deserves further investigation. As contemporary media-theoretical accounts of computation have suggested, there is a crucial “ontological difference” between computation as a broader material-cosmological operation and the computer-regulated networks of communication and control it has become narrowly associated with. In this view, the latter does not exhaust the powerful (and potentially liberatory) ontogenetic potentials that modern-day computational technologies have unleashed, despite capitalist capture, and which grant 21st-century humans unprecedented access to the originary creativity of the universe and a world-sensibility beyond anthropocentric norms and forms of intelligibility.

My current research seeks to work through this tension by examining the social ontology of computation. Only by grasping the human forms of relation at the basis of modern computational systems, I contend, can the distinction between computation as capitalist technique and as worldly process be properly drawn.

If social ontology, as broadly understood in its contemporary articulations from Lukács to systems theory, is chiefly concerned with the modes of being of the social, the social ontology of a technical system ought to account for the intersubjective human processes at the core of its genesis and self-actualization. On this basis, my working hypothesis is that contemporary computational systems unfold from the industrial form of the labor process, understood as a historically distinct arrangement of the interface between humans and machines that arose at the dawn of Western modernity. The term “industrial” refers, in this context, not to the specific technologies and industries of a bygone Victorian or Fordist era, now seemingly superseded by a post-industrial world, but to a distinct _formal_ configuration of human metabolic activity, consisting of large-scale production of commodified goods and services for the purpose of expanding capital. What characterizes this industrial form is not, as the common understanding would have it, the productive employment of humans, machines, and nature for maximum physical efficiency, but rather the measurement and allocation of human labor-time -- and the associated operationalization of the division of labor -- in the most value-efficient way. 

As recent scholarship has argued, the twin birth of this industrial arrangement and the computer points to a common operational logic. Indeed, if the industrial coupling of humans and machines necessitates a systematic regimentation of labor-time and value production, then computation is the mechanism by which the modern technical system makes this requirement actionable and enforceable at scale. What is more, the imperative that this informational process become ever more granular, recursive, and operative across the whole technical system means that it must eventually become inscribed in the self-regulation of its parts. What thereby emerges is a machinic species of intelligence increasingly severed from human agency and embodiment, whose genesis is not reducible to deskilling, workers’ resistance, the rising organic composition of capital, or any of the structural imperatives driving its immediate development. Rather, as the most realized form of universal computation, artificial intelligence inheres in the social world of capitalist modernity. The union of word and number represented by large language models, and their audacious promise to automate vast swaths of the global economy, is only its latest articulation.

Contemporary computational systems are, in sum, the crystallization of a mode of social cooperation based on the universal commensuration of human activity by way of the commodity form of the product of labor. Yet as a technical tendency, computation cannot be equated with reification, as though it were exhausted by the alienated form of human valuation that the capital relation represents. In fact, an alternative, non-alienated system of social cooperation based on the universality of human needs and capacities similarly implies a commensurating (albeit consciously mediated) system for comparing and reconciling non-identical contents, where computational technologies would serve human flourishing rather than the perpetuation of a self-moving abstraction. In that regard, the world-disclosing possibilities that contemporary computational systems already exhibit, even when enfolded in a reified social world, are but an adumbration of a form of human individuation that does not yet exist.
